What is Club Alpha?

α

Why Alpha?

In investing, alpha is the value you create beyond what was expected — the return above the benchmark, above what the market would have produced on its own. In Club Alpha, you'll learn how founders and investors figure out whether an idea can become a real business. Then you'll investigate real problems, test your assumptions, understand the numbers, build something people want, and defend your thinking.

By February, you won't just have an idea. You'll know whether it's worth betting on.

Alpha, explained

A race with the wind at your back.

Imagine a school race. A strong wind pushes every runner forward. Everyone gets faster, even without trying.

In the race

The wind

It is the market. Everyone gets its help. It is free.

In the race

Alpha

How much faster you run than the wind alone would make you. It comes from your own effort and smart choices.

In startups

A venture capitalist

Someone who picks which new companies to help. If they pick winners that others miss, they have alpha.

In startups

A founder

A founder has alpha when their company grows faster than other companies in the same market.

Check yourself

If the wind helps everyone win, is that alpha?

No. Alpha is what you do beyond the wind.

Alpha means: I did better because of my own skill, not just because of the wind.

The program

Five months. One business. Built from scratch.

Club Alpha is a five-month student founder development program for Birmingham scholars, running October through February and culminating in Pitch Day at Startup Weekend. Scholars learn the difference between a small business and a startup, why that difference decides how each one gets funded, and which one they actually want to build.

Weekly

Virtual club

Monthly

In-person workshop

Every scholar

A Claude account

February

Startup Weekend

Full event participation, ending on the pitch stage in front of a room.

AI, used properly

Scholars use AI the way working founders and analysts use it — as a research and building tool, not a shortcut. They run customer interviews, size a market, build a prototype, and defend real numbers with it, and they leave fluent in the tools a business setting will expect them to already know.

The arc

A business of your own, from week one.

The arc is the one a real founder walks — in order, because the order is the lesson.

01

Thesis

Write a problem, a solution, and a hypothesis you can be wrong about.

02

Evidence

Run qualitative and quantitative research, including live customer interviews, and change your mind when the data says to.

03

Economics

Learn what makes a business profitable — margins, unit economics, what a customer costs to acquire against what they're worth, and how to size a market honestly.

04

Category

Understand the difference between a small business and a startup, and choose deliberately instead of by default.

05

Build

Get hands on a prototype and the AI tools to make one fast.

06

Defend

Stand in front of a room and answer hard questions about your own numbers.

Not a watered-down simulation of entrepreneurship. The language, mental models, technology, and expectations of an actual professional business environment — and then how to operate inside it.

What scholars leave with

Three things, and one of them isn't on a résumé.

01

A business that's market-ready

Not a worksheet and not a poster board. A real idea, tested against real people, with numbers behind it.

02

AI fluency employers are hiring for

Built by using the tools for real work over five months, not by watching a demo.

03

A working investor's eye

The instinct to ask what makes a company worth backing — and the method to find out.

Most importantly, they leave knowing where they want to start their entrepreneurial journeys and holding the tools to get there.

February

pitch day

By February, you'll have the tools to investigate any business and find out whether it makes money, how it makes money, and what would have to change for it to grow. Those are the same questions an investor at an investment firm asks before they put money into a company.

And get ready — because you'll be asking them about your own business idea. On Pitch Day in February, you and your team stand in front of a room and defend the startup you've built in the program.
